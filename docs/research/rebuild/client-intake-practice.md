# Client intake: what professionals actually capture — practices worth encoding into the role contract

consumer: commands/intake.md — cited by the intake door for the interview craft + right-sizing tiers

_Research brief, 2026-07-13 — input to the non-spine rebuild TSOW (task #3/#10). Web-researched,
source-grounded; distilled for the design session._

Framing: across agency playbooks (Automattic for Agencies, Digital Applied), freelancing guides (Jennifer Bourn, Content Snare, Prismic, Local Creative), and discovery literature (GOV.UK Service Manual, Erika Hall's *Just Enough Research*, thoughtbot's Playbook), the same discipline recurs: **understand before proposing, bound before building, sign before starting.** That maps directly onto friday's constitutional principle.

## Practices worth encoding

1. **Ask "why this project" before "what to build."** Every serious questionnaire opens with business goals and the desired outcome, not features — features are means, not ends. (Jennifer Bourn; thoughtbot Playbook)
2. **Capture the standing dimensions every time: goals, audience, existing content/assets, technical needs, budget, timeline, ongoing maintenance.** These are the seven areas professional intake forms always cover; skipping one is where projects go sideways. (Content Snare's 25-question intake; DesignRush)
3. **Get the budget range up front and treat it as a design input.** Pros ask early because the right recommendation depends on it — you spec to the budget, not the other way around. (Preface Studios discovery guide)
4. **Reality-check the timeline against the client's "why now."** Ask when they need results and why; comparable builds run six weeks to six months, so mismatched expectations must surface at intake, not delivery. (Preface Studios)
5. **Name the decision-maker, and ask "who else must approve?"** A surprise stakeholder giving late feedback is a classic project killer; pros ask for one owner with authority and a named approver list at intake. (Local Creative's discovery-call questions)
6. **Write down what is explicitly NOT included, not just what is.** The out-of-scope list (extra pages, copywriting, maintenance, third-party integrations) is the single most effective — and most commonly missing — anti-scope-creep tool. (Digital Applied SOW framework)
7. **Bound every deliverable with counts and revision limits.** "Three page designs, two revision rounds each" is a scope; "page designs" is an argument waiting to happen. (Digital Applied)
8. **Pre-agree a change process at intake.** When new requests arrive, "that's outside scope — I'll price a change order" only works if the mechanism was agreed before it was needed; only ~1% of agencies successfully bill out-of-scope work without one. (Digital Applied; Sakas & Company)
9. **Record the client's own words for the problem and for success.** Ask naive open questions ("why do you do it that way?"), ask the same question of multiple people to expose disagreement, and keep an explicit "what we know vs. what we assume" list. (Erika Hall, *Just Enough Research*)
10. **Define measurable success criteria before ending discovery.** Discovery isn't done until you know what data will show the thing is working. (GOV.UK Service Manual, "How the discovery phase works")
11. **Inventory accounts and access on day one: domain, hosting, analytics, email, social, brand files.** Collecting access is the step agencies most often underestimate; request everything together, before production starts. (Automattic for Agencies onboarding checklist)
12. **Probe ownership, not just access.** "Who is the domain registered to?" is a structural red flag — assets registered under a previous vendor's name can hold the whole project hostage. (NorthMac Services, "Do you own your website?")
13. **Assign content responsibility contractually, with a deadline clause.** Client-supplied content is the most notorious delay in the trade ("every single project was backed up because of content"); pros write "work starts when content arrives" or price in a copywriter. (Content Snare)
14. **Sign off the brief/SOW before any work begins; sign off each phase before the next.** The written, mutually approved document is the boundary between discovery and delivery — held informally: rapport notes, working preferences, raw interview material. Held formally and signed: goals, scope, exclusions, price, timeline, approver. (Ziflow/Workamajig creative-brief guides; Digital Applied)

## Right-sizing the intake to the engagement (friday field lesson)

_Discovery must be proportionate — over-scoping a one-person business is its own
failure mode. Salvaged from the pre-rebuild intake command (D-0041)._

- **Sole trader / 1–2 person:** business discovery = goals / audience / logistics
  (~10–15 questions); **omit** detailed brand discovery, technical/CMS deep-dives,
  and competitor analysis. Compress the commercial frame to ownership + payment +
  exit.
- **Small business:** the full pass, lightly.
- **Larger:** the full pass, plus the heavier compliance / disaster-recovery / SLA
  detail.

## AI pre-derives vs. interview-only

An AI expert can legitimately pre-derive: industry norms, competitor landscape, technical options and their tradeoffs, standard deliverable templates, and the red-flag checklist itself. Only the client interview can supply: the real budget, the real deadline and its reason, who decides, what they already own (accounts, content, brand assets), their vocabulary for the problem, and what "success" means to them. The interview's job is the second list — the AI's pre-work should sharpen the questions, never substitute for the answers. (Consistent with Erika Hall's know-vs-assume discipline and GDS's "learn the constraints before proposing.")
